*(Context: this is one of the first parts of many in which I attempt to construct a new perception of morality: not morality itself, but the correct way to project authenticity and freedom. It is excruciatingly constrained and simplified due to page limits, and does not fully demonstrate the scale of philosophical ideal, but nevertheless this is an attempt to be examined.)*

> "The unexamined life is not worth living" — Socrates

There is, in a sense, a contradiction at the center of modern life. The causal worldview, often unnoticed, sees human action as inevitable, articulations of antecedent conditions, shadowed by the Goliath of material. As such, responsibility, the basis of our moral system, any language of "ought", "should", begins to seem like fiction. Yet we continue to seek to justify, determine, argue about "should" and "shouldn't". What is our reason for this? Can this be justified? Does life just boil down to some social theatre performed by puppets tugged on by the strings of physical laws? The stakes of the debate of free will are grim; what lies on the line, quite literally, is the intelligibility and meaning of all moral life. From here on out, any attempt to preserve moral seriousness at all must be done without importing need for any form of a "leap of faith" if it is to be done in good faith. If this proof is to have authority at all, it must be grounded in reason itself, not convenience. I call the task that remains undistortion: the discipline of building true freedom through reason; the self under examination.

There is, of course, first something we must establish: the rationale of determinism, or a causal worldview. An uninviting proof, yet unfortunately necessary. The world does not tolerate exemptions to physical law simply because we find them morally convenient, and if human beings are as all other things are, subject to logic, then choice is similarly subject to logic; in this case previous circumstances such as temperament, memory, language, fatigue, fear, and other conditions that shape will in the moment of choice. (To understand the rigorous proof of this claim, a formal statement appears in [appendix A](#appendix-a).)

This is evident in our birth, or the formation of consciousness. A decision passes through a "true self", our character. But there is no initial "character", a being without input of experience is simply a bundle of genes, yet the experiences that shape us arrive before any form of self awareness. Later, we gain ability to revise ourselves, but the way we think, the way we refine ourselves is authored by objective conditions we did not control. This is why the deterministic suspicion cannot be dismissed as mere cynicism; by logic, it shapes our agency, and if we accept it as true, it is a strike to the heart for our view of freedom: evidently, if our personality and choices can simply be reduced to scientific phenomena, how can choice have any meaning to it? How can we be held responsible?

Fortunately, under closer examination, the rationale of the idea does not by any means collapse into a fatalistic attitude. The causal picture does not threaten meaning, but a certain interpretation of freedom and responsibility, the idea that moral weight requires an unconditioned relation between an entity and the way they act.

The most common picture of freedom, the one under siege, is imagined as unconditioned origination. In this view, a choice is mine only if it rises from a part of me untouched by any outer conditions, some inner point of pure spontaneity. But this ideal is a philosophical unfit and unattainable. The process of existing being uncaused would not give ownership, only relinquish any substance to existence: it would make you a blob of metaphysical goo that doesn't really have any essence besides the definition of the concept that it is supposed to embody, a paradox. Being caused by yourself, of course, is also logically impossible, as it goes against the laws of time and physics. This fantasy, though under closer examination clearly absurd, is widely accepted and has significant moral consequences, leading to misinformation at best and existential death at worst, encouraging an evasion for those who want to escape responsibility, treating any influence as exoneration, cause as coercion. This type of thought ought to be condemned; we must accept that to be human is not to be the uncaused origin of oneself; it is to be the kind of creature for whom justification makes sense.

The requirement of the uncaused, of course, is not what morality presupposes. What is core here is not some sort of metaphysical exemption of universal rules, we examine whether an action issues from a person's capacities to understand, weigh, and answer to considerations, whether it is undistorted; whether the agent can recognize and revise themselves in light of it. Under this examination, freedom should instead be defined as rational self-governance, the causal picture the very stage that allows responsibility to exist.

Rational self-governance names a kind of freedom that does not compete with causality but specifies a form of control within it. An agent governs themselves when their action issues from capacities of rationale: they can grasp the relevant considerations, weigh them against competing incentives, and, most importantly, revise their judgment when the better reason is made clear. On this view, freedom is the intelligibility from deliberation to action: the person's evaluative standpoint is something through which the action is produced. A free action, then, is not uncaused; it is an act that expresses the agent's character in its rational aspect, an act that can be held to moral judgment.

A useful test is counterfactual: if stronger reasons had been present, such as a decisive moral consideration, would the agent's deliberative capacities have been able to register them and adjust? Responsibility lies in that sensitivity, where it is systematically blocked, responsibility is diminished not because causation is present, but because governance is not.

A familiar objection insists that determinism is simply coercion at its maximum volume, as under it, we really can't have different outcomes. However, this conflates two different ideas. This objection gains its force by misunderstanding two senses of "could not have done otherwise." Under determinism, the claim is conditional: given the exact same total conditions, the same action follows. Under coercion, the claim states that the action follows because the agent's rational control is overridden—if a man held a gun to my head, I would comply with his demand whether I deemed the act moral or not. Determinism does not, by itself, bypass anything, cloud any judgement. In fact, if determinism were itself coercion, the very concept of coercion would dissolve, as we would lose the moral difference between "judging something acceptable" and "being forced." Coercion explains when the route from reason to action has been hijacked, and if determinism erased that difference, coercion would cease to be a concept at all; it would name everything and therefore explain nothing.

To better understand this conception of freedom, consider a simple action: a deliberate lie told to escape inconvenience. Under determinism, the lie is easily explained using materialism; it is caused by the neurons flowing through our mind at the moment, which caused our momentary mental state, our emotions and rationale, the previous actions that caused the neurons to move that way, we could go all the way back to the big bang. None of that settles the moral question, whether the lie is issued from capacities that could recognize the relevant reasons (that deception uses another person as a means; that trust is being exploited), and whether the agent's deliberation was the mechanism by which those reasons were ignored or overridden. When those capacities are intact, blame makes sense: it symbolizes a conclusion of mismatch between what the agent could recognize as warranted and what they did after rational processing, which is what morality should denounce.

If blame is not a verdict on the uncaused, then our moral life must be rewritten in a different key. What determinism removes is the hallucination of an inner metaphysical "opening" that makes praise and blame absolute, a system that attributes all the causes and reasons to ourselves and ourselves only. It forces us to admit that inevitability is not rare; it is the background of everything, meaning our moral distinctions cannot be grounded in a fantasy of metaphysical openness, but in something that survives necessity: whether an action ran through the agent's capacity to answer to reasons, or whether that capacity was bypassed.

This revises accountability structurally. Moral judgment becomes less like a verdict about what a person is and more like an assessment of how agency functioned. We are no longer licensed to treat blame as though the wrongdoer authored their own soul out of nothing. This makes moral life stricter in a new sense. Determinism forces a revision to blame; it revises what we must become for any "should" to have authority at all. Freedom, after this revision, is not the uncaused but the undistorted: the extent to which deliberation is governed by reasons — how thoroughly reason has cleansed coercion. On this account, the moral significance of a decision depends on whether the agent's self under reason was actually governing, as that is the condition of answerability, thus moral meaning: that the will is examined under reason. As such, failure to act under reason when available signals a conscious abandoning of pursuit of meaning, a stark rejection of responsibility, and therefore of moral meaning. It must be clarified, if not clear enough, here the duty is not to be objectively perfectly rational, but to refuse the comfort of avoidable distortion when reason is genuinely available.

The second revision develops cohesively: the moral problem becomes a question of discipline. The moral problem is no longer merely "Which code is correct?" but "How can systems be proved under causality, and can they be reached through means of reason?" Under necessity, moral seriousness cannot be inherited or performed; it has to be earned through the discipline of rationalization, by examination.

Thereafter, the center of moral judgment shifts. When we call an act wrong, we are not merely distributing condemnation; we are implicitly judging the governance, or lack thereof of the stance behind it. When we prosecute an action, two situations are possible: either it was the product of coercion due to laziness of thought or the product of coercion forcefully placed. We must differentiate the two, as in the first situation, not reaching rationale was an active decision, showing intent to stray from morality; rationale is defined as by logic, and therefore to reach rationale it takes effort only as logic is the inherent way we understand the world. Such indolence is morally corrosive, it shows a fault, a subtle fear of morality. The danger is not only that we do ill; it is that we learn to justify inaction; inaction of thought, inaction of examination, of revision. There is but one situation where moral consequence are excused: when one is coerced without ability to escape, when there is mandatory bypass of rational institution. When we examine this system of blame, determinism forces a harsher honesty, stripped of metaphysical, romantic idealism: moral progress is measured not by how "good" we judge ourselves to be, but by how "authentic" the justifications of the decisions we make are, as "should" thus stops becoming a location, but method.

This is why the path of man cannot mean simply becoming "more moral," Morality is not some "mountain" one can reach and simply climb up using a set of rules already set. It means having more meaningful choice by the process of purification of coercion. The task is not to invent oneself from nothing, but to take responsibility for the self one has been given (the character that develops before self awareness): to train the capacities that make morals genuinely one's own. Any attempt to become moral before becoming rational, then, is pitifully misguided: morality and rationality are intertwined; two faces of the same coin, both required for each other's existence.

Therefore, accountability becomes less like throwing a stone and more like forcing a mirror into place: it forces man to accept the natural-born responsibility we have as well as right. In this framework, "understanding" is precision, it is rationale, it is the premise to our will to morality, it is the refusal to treat explanation as excuse, the refusal to give into a thoughtless and therefore meaningless existence.

And once morality is procedural in this sense, punishment and praise are no longer miracles. They both become sharper: we no longer praise one because they truly author their experience, which is assumed by the prior system, but as though we are recognizing the difficult responsibility that one chooses to bear; the kind that is natural as a human: building authenticity that came to be ruled by reasons rather than ruled by impulse. Punishment, similarly, becomes condemnation towards the refusal of thought, the rejection of the great human burden, rejection to realize oneself, rejection of the "true rationale" at the core of any character.

Under this view, there is the start of the system, with human life split into two movements; formation and examination. Formation is the beginning: the self is built before it is chosen. It spans over our childhood, with our experience and our bundle of genes gradually going through the biological process of authoring character, already distorted in small way that cannot yet comprehend. Then, there is examination: the long process of developing answerability, to become truly free by constant undistortion. To undistort within condition is to examine the irrationalities that lie in the self and to submit them to reason. Even if the first self is immoral by default, as it is unchosen, it is still subject to reason, subject to rationalization, to change. That is why the unexamined life is not merely shallow but also unfree: it lets irrationality rule while pretending it is the self. The choices made are meaningless, for they are made by judgement clouded with distortion. The examined life is the opposite. It has the perception of morality as method; crafting character every conscious moment like some metaphysical blacksmith pounding away with the hammer of reason—getting ever closer to the final self; the true self. It is here that humanity finds the ultimate goal: The great undistortion; to finally purify oneself of irrationality, to reach ultimate meaning and answerability through the rational method, to finally understand the meaning of oneself, of concept, to conclude the quest for morality, reaching an authentic answer.

As thus, determinism does not return us to the old moral world; it purifies it. It removes the metaphysical shortcuts and mental gymnastics of evasion. It forces us to locate moral seriousness where it actually lives: it is to build reason in which morality has relevance.

Once morality is aimed at governance, the old hunger for metaphysical repayment begins to look like a well-dressed superstition.

What loses its glamour is the punitive fantasy of responsibility as pure desert, as though wrongdoing generates an intrinsic metaphysical debt that must be repaid simply because the will "could have done otherwise" in some unconditioned sense. Once freedom is understood as governance, moral practice is intelligible again, rising anew beneath the ashes of cosmic threat.

There is the resolution of the initial contradiction, and new substance. A world governed by necessity need not be a world emptied of normativity. Determinism need not, can not reduce moral life to theater; it instead clarifies morality by addressing persons as beings who can be guided by reasons, holding them answerable when they refuse what they can recognize, the duty that is placed upon them. The world may be causally closed, but it need not be silent. Reasons belong to the world precisely because they belong to creatures who can understand them, contest them, and are capable of a never-ending quest under the pressure of them — change.

## Appendix A: Formal Statement of Macro-Determinism {#appendix-a}

### Definition

**MD:** For an agent at time *t*, a complete specification of the macro-state relevant to agency (psychological/neurobiological state at the level of beliefs, attention, habit, incentives, stress/arousal, etc.) plus the relevant macro-level dynamics fixes (settles) the agent's action at *t*+Δ.

### Key terms

- **Macro-state M<sub>t</sub>:** the coarse-grained state relevant to decision (not microphysics).
- **Macro-dynamics F:** the stable mapping from macro-state + input → next macro-state, as described by neurobiology/psychology.
- **Robustness:** small micro-variations don't change the macro outcome.

### Premises

**P1. Realization (agency is physical):** Every choice-relevant mental state (beliefs, desires, intentions, attention, evaluations) is realized in the agent's physical system, and is captured (for explanatory purposes) by some macro-state M<sub>t</sub>.

**P2. Macro-causal sufficiency:** At the timescale of choice, the next macro-state is a function of the current macro-state and macro-inputs:

M<sub>t+Δ</sub> = F(M<sub>t</sub>, I<sub>t</sub>)

where I<sub>t</sub> includes environment/information/social pressure, etc.

**P3. Determinacy of the macro-map:** For a given M<sub>t</sub> and I<sub>t</sub>, F yields a unique next macro-state (no branching at the macro level):

(M<sub>t</sub>, I<sub>t</sub>) ⇒ exactly one M<sub>t+Δ</sub>

**P4. Robustness under micro-noise:** Any micro-indeterminacy (randomness at the micro-level) does not systematically alter the macro transition under normal conditions; it either averages out or is absorbed without changing the macro outcome. So F remains effectively single-valued at the macro scale.

**P5. Action supervenes on macro-state:** The action at *t*+Δ, call it A<sub>t+Δ</sub>, is fixed by the macro-state at *t*+Δ:

A<sub>t+Δ</sub> = G(M<sub>t+Δ</sub>)

with G yielding a unique action given the relevant state.

### Conclusion

From P2–P4, M<sub>t+Δ</sub> is uniquely fixed by M<sub>t</sub> and I<sub>t</sub>.

From P5, A<sub>t+Δ</sub> is uniquely fixed by M<sub>t+Δ</sub>.

Therefore, A<sub>t+Δ</sub> is uniquely fixed by M<sub>t</sub> and I<sub>t</sub>. So MD holds.
